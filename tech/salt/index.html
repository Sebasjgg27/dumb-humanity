Salt: Harvesting & Trade

Salt: Harvesting & Trade

Salt is food preservation’s partner, physiology’s necessity (sweat loss), and history’s first trade commodity. A community with a salt source has a currency. Before currency, salt was currency — which is where the word “salary” comes from.

Sources

  1. Seawater — ~3.5% salt. The classic.
  2. Brine springs/wells — ancient salt deposits dissolved in groundwater.
  3. Rock salt (halite) — old mines and natural outcrops.
  4. Salt plants — plants that concentrate salt (glasswort); burn them to ash for a salty residue (used even in modern survival).

Solar salt pans (no fuel)

  1. Shallow clay-lined basins filled with seawater.
  2. Let the sun and wind evaporate.
  3. Rake off the white crystals. Rain ruins a batch — cover or drain fast.

Boiling brine (fuel-hungry but reliable)

  • Boil brine in shallow pans until crystals form, then low-and-slow to avoid burning.
  • Works anywhere; costs a lot of firewood. Use solar first, boil as backup.

Purity & iodine

  • Iodine is essential (thyroid, brain development). Modern salt is iodized — scavenged iodized salt is a health goldmine. Sea salt has some iodine; inland rock salt has none.
  • Wash away bitter impurities (magnesium salts) by draining the first crystals.

Salt as currency & value

  • Salt preserves meat/fish, which is why communities with access could trade it.
  • A salt surplus is one of the first real economic assets a settlement has.
  • Historically worth its weight in gold at some points — the economics follow preservation value.

Validation checklist

  • Can you harvest salt from your local environment without modern inputs?
  • Do you have a salt-based food preservation routine?
  • Do you know whether your salt provides enough iodine for the group?
  • Do you have a surplus for trade?
