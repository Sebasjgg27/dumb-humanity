Agriculture: From Gathering to Growing

Agriculture: From Gathering to Growing

Agriculture is the first act of civilization: converting hunting luck into predictable calories. It also creates the surplus that frees people from constant food work — the surplus that makes specialists (smiths, chemists, teachers) possible. This is where community becomes possible.

Start small, start right

One person can dig and plant enough for themselves with a digging stick. Don’t overreach in year one — a failed large field is worse than a successful small one.

Seed strategy (the most important long-term asset)

  1. Save seed from every harvest — the seed stock is worth more than the food.
  2. Choose open-pollinated/heirloom varieties; hybrids don’t reproduce true.
  3. Store seed dry and cool; test germination before planting season.
  4. Plant a seed-saving plot separate from the eating plot, and let it fully ripen.
  5. Diversify: a multi-crop field survives pests and weather that kill a monoculture.

The four-field basics

  • Rotation — don’t plant the same crop in the same ground; pests and disease build up.
  • Companion planting — corn + beans (nitrogen-fixer) + squash is the classic “Three Sisters” design that works everywhere.
  • Fertility without fertilizer: compost, animal manure, ash, legumes, green manure.
  • Water discipline — near a water source, or in a rain-catching hollow. Mulch (a layer of straw/leaves) cuts water loss hugely.

Tools: wooden → iron

The digging stick → hoe → iron hoe → plow is the tool ladder within agriculture. Each upgrade multiplies how much ground one person can work. Iron tools come from the iron smelting node — and agriculture is exactly why smithing pays for itself.

The calendar is infrastructure

Write down: frost dates, planting windows, harvest dates. A written agricultural calendar is more valuable than any single tool, because knowledge compounds across seasons.

Validation checklist

  • Can you produce a reliable calorie surplus from one small plot?
  • Are you saving seed every harvest?
  • Do you have a written planting/harvest calendar for your climate?
  • Have you chosen crops that thrive in your specific soil and season?
