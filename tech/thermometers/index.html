Thermometers

Temperature is invisible, so we cheat: liquids expand predictably when heated. A sealed tube of liquid with a scale turns heat into a number — and numbers let chemistry, medicine, and metallurgy be repeatable. Before the thermometer, every recipe was a guess.

Why it matters on the ladder

  • Chemistry: most reactions need a temperature window. Sulfuric acid, soap-making, and sugar work all run hotter or cooler than hand-feel.
  • Medicine: fever detection decides quarantine and care.
  • Food safety: the difference between pasteurized and spoiled.
  • Metallurgy: steel heat-treatment (temper colors) is temperature control by eye — a thermometer makes it reliable.

How it works

Liquid expands more than glass when heated, so the column rises in a sealed capillary tube. Two fixed points calibrate the scale:

  1. Freezing water = 0 °C (or 32 °F).
  2. Boiling water = 100 °C (or 212 °F).

Mark the tube at both, divide the gap evenly, and you have a scale — the same numbers every civilization uses.

Building one

  1. Blow a bulb at one end of a glass tube and a narrow capillary above it (see glassblowing).
  2. Fill the bulb with mercury (dense, consistent, wide range) or dyed alcohol (safer, cheaper, but lower boiling point). Filling requires heating the bulb and drawing liquid in through the open end — a practiced glassmaker’s trick.
  3. Seal the top after filling, leaving a small bubble so expansion has room.
  4. Calibrate against freezing and boiling water; scratch marks and a scale.
  5. Protect it in a wooden or metal frame.

Alternatives at lower tech

  • Galileo thermoscope: a sealed flask whose air volume changes with temperature — cruder, but shows hot vs cold.
  • Bimetallic strip (later): two metals expanding differently bend a pointer — the basis of oven dials.

Handling and safety

  • Mercury is toxic — it is not “silver water.” Never heat a mercury thermometer over flame directly; use a water bath. If it breaks, collect every drop carefully; don’t let children near it.
  • Alcohol thermometers are the safe default for general use.
  • Glass thermometers are fragile — store them in a padded tube.

Validation checklist

  • Can you produce a sealed liquid thermometer?
  • Is it calibrated to the freezing and boiling points of water?
  • Can you measure fever, water temperature, and workshop temperatures?
  • Do you handle mercury safely (or use alcohol instead)?
