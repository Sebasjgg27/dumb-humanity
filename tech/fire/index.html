Fire Making (Bow Drill & Scavenge)

Fire Making

Fire is the first technology. It extends daylight, purifies water, preserves food, hardens tools, and — on this ladder — it produces the charcoal that boots all of A2 industry.

First: scavenge before you craft

Modern ruins are fire starters. Check ruins before spending hours on a bow drill: lighters, matchbooks, magnesium/ferro rods, lens glasses, steel wool + battery, or a hand crank generator. One good ferro rod = months of reliable fire if the spark training is solid.

The bow drill, done right

Components:

  • Bow: a curved green stick, ~arm’s length. Cordage strung loose.
  • Drill (spindle): a straight dry stick, ~2cm diameter, ~20–25cm long. Softwood (cedar, willow, cottonwood) drills faster than hardwood.
  • Fireboard: a dry board, ~1.5cm thick, same softwood family as the drill.
  • Bearing block: hardwood or stone with a socket, held in your off-hand to press down on the drill top.
  • Tinder bundle: bone-dry, finely fibrous material (bark shavings, dry grass, char cloth) held in a loose nest.

Steps:

  1. Cut a notch in the fireboard, down to the middle of a socket hole.
  2. Place char cloth or a tinder feather directly under the notch.
  3. Kneel with one foot on the board. Apply downward pressure with the bearing block and saw the bow slowly at first, then fast and steady.
  4. The goal is a pile of brown, smoking dust at the notch – not a full flame.
  5. Transfer the smoking dust to the tinder nest. Gently cup it, blow steadily, and feed the ember until the nest bursts into flame.
  6. Add small kindling, then larger wood. Fire triangle: fuel, heat, oxygen.

Common failures:

  • Cord too loose or too taut (adjust tension).
  • Wrong woods (soft + soft is slow; soft drill + soft board same species is best).
  • Not enough pressure on the bearing block.
  • Rushing the tinder transfer.

Keeping fire

  • Bank it at night: cover coals with ash, keep a channel open. A fire that “sleeps” saves hours of relighting.
  • Fire mound: dry wood stacked under a raised rock platform or metal sheet keeps tinder dry in rain.
  • Char cloth in a tin: smolders reliably, easy to carry as ember starter.

Fire for industry (preview)

Fire’s most important legacy is not heat — it’s charcoal, the pure-carbon fuel that reaches the temperatures needed for pottery glazing, glass, and iron smelting. See the charcoal guide.

Safety

  • Clear a 2m ring of flammable material.
  • Water within reach. Never leave it unattended.
  • In shelters, ensure smoke can escape (CO poisoning kills silently).
  • Never sleep with an unvented fire indoors.

Validation checklist

  • Can you start a fire with a bow drill in under 15 minutes?
  • Can you keep it alive for 12 hours?
  • Can you safely bank it overnight and relight from coals?
  • Do you know the scavenge sources for fire starters in ruins?
