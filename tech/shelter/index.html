Emergency Shelter

Emergency Shelter

Shelter is protection from the elements, not luxury. Priorities in order: insulation from the ground (the biggest heat loss), wind block, then rain cover. A person dies faster from exposure than hunger.

The ground is the enemy

  • Lying directly on cold ground strips body heat relentlessly. Always insulate below: a 30cm layer of leaves, pine boughs, dry grass, or a scavenged blanket.
  • 80% of the thermal battle is won by the ground layer alone.

The lean-to

  1. Find two trees ~2m apart, or build two forked stakes.
  2. Lay a ridgepole between them, waist height.
  3. Lean branches against it at 45–60°, overlapping like shingles.
  4. Layer leaves/bark/grass on top — thickest at the top where rain runs down.
  5. Wall the sides; keep the opening facing away from the wind.
  6. Ground insulation + a small fire reflector (a second lean-to across the fire) can heat the whole structure.

The debris hut

For no-tools survival: a ridgeline tunnel of interlocked branches, packed 30cm thick with dry leaves. Small opening, tight fit, huge insulation. Build it before you need it — a debris hut is a 1–2 hour job.

Selecting a site

  • High ground, away from flash-flood washes and dead trees (widowmakers).
  • Near water and firewood, but not in the path of runoff.
  • Sun exposure matters: morning sun is a free heater.

Windproofing in practice

  • Shelters collapse more from wind than weight. Lash the ridgeline, don’t just rest it.
  • A tarp or scavenged sheet dramatically out-performs natural thatch in rain — use it.

Validation checklist

  • Can you build a dry, windproof shelter in under 2 hours using only hand tools?
  • Is your sleeping area insulated from the ground by at least 20cm?
  • Does it survive a rainstorm without leaking on your bedding?
  • Can two people build and sleep in it?
