First Aid & Wound Care

In a reboot, infection kills more people than injury. The single most valuable medical skill is cleanliness — because you cannot synthesize antibiotics until A4, but you can prevent most infections with water, soap, and boiled cloth. Every wound is a potential infection, and every infection is a potential death.

The golden sequence

  1. Stop the bleeding – direct pressure, elevate, pressure bandage.
  2. Clean the wound – the most important step. Boiled (cooled) water, soap if you have it. Rinse thoroughly, remove visible debris.
  3. Close if needed – butterfly strips, clean cloth strips, or interrupted sutures.
  4. Dress and keep dry – change dressing daily; wet dressings breed infection.
  5. Watch – redness spreading, heat, pus, fever = infection. Treat aggressively.

Improvised wound wash

  • Boiled water cooled to body temperature is your sterile saline equivalent.
  • Honey is a genuine topical antibacterial (osmotic effect) — one of the oldest dressings and still effective.

Burns

  • Cool with cool running water for 20 minutes (not ice — causes tissue damage).
  • Don’t pop blisters. Cover loosely with clean cloth.
  • Bad sign: charred skin, numbness, no pain = full thickness. Medical care needed.

Fractures & splinting

  • Splint the joint above and below the break. Pad well.
  • Improvised splints: boards, rolled newspaper, a folded blanket + cord.
  • Never try to straighten a deformed limb unless circulation is threatened.

The scavenged pharmacy — rationing logic

  • Antibiotics: a precious finite resource. Use for infections, not colds. Don’t share doses — incomplete courses breed resistance. When they’re gone, they’re gone.
  • Painkillers: store and ration. Do not “prevent” pain, treat it.
  • Suture kits, antiseptics, bandages: hoard and protect from moisture.
  • Tag every vial with what it is and the date you know it from.

When you are the clinic

  • Keep a “treat first” list: bleeding > breathing > circulation > hypothermia > wound.
  • Run the ABCDE sweep before any fancy care.
  • Triage in a group: treat those who can be saved with limited resources first.

Validation checklist

  • Can you stop arterial bleeding with improvised materials?
  • Can you clean, close, and dress a wound to prevent infection?
  • Can you splint a fracture?
  • Do you know which scavenged drugs to ration and why?
