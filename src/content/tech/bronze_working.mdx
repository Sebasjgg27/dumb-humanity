# Bronze & Alloying

Alloying is the leap from "one metal" to "designing materials." Bronze (copper + tin)
was so valuable it named an entire age — and the *principle* — mixing metals to change
their properties — is the seed of all metallurgy that follows.

## The alloy recipe

- **Bronze**: 88–92% copper + 8–12% tin. Softer than iron but casts far better
  (flows into molds, sharp edges, low melting point ~950°C).
- **Brass**: copper + zinc (~15–30%). Corrosion-resistant, good for fittings.
- **Steel**: iron + carbon (0.2–2%). See [steel](/dumb-humanity/tech/steel_making).

## Why alloy at all?

- Pure copper is too soft for tools; pure tin is too weak.
- Mixing adds hardness, lowers melting point, or improves casting — you *design*
  the material for the job. This mindset is the core of the entire A2/A3 materials science.

## Tin: the bottleneck

- Tin ore (cassiterite) is scarce and geographically specific — that's why ancient
  bronze trade routes stretched thousands of miles.
- Alternative alloyants: arsenic (dangerous, poisonous fumes) or antimony (rare).
- **Strategy**: when tin is unavailable, bronze is a luxury — use iron for tools and
  reserve bronze for fittings, bearings, and castings.

## Casting basics

1. Clay/sand mold in two halves with a sprue (pour hole) and vent.
2. Dry the mold completely — steam in wet clay = violent failure.
3. Pour molten bronze steadily; let it cool slowly to avoid cracking.
4. File/grind the sprue off. That's the whole craft, repeated well.
