# Compass (Magnetic)

A magnetized needle aligns with Earth's magnetic field and points north. That single
fact ends the era of "follow the river and hope": with a compass you can hold a course,
map a region, and build everything from roads to rail that must run straight.

## Why it matters on the ladder

- **Navigation**: travel and trade beyond sight of home becomes reliable.
- **Surveying**: laying out buildings, roads, fields, and boundaries on a true bearing.
- **Orientation**: aligning buildings to light and weather.
- **A proof of magnetism**, which later becomes the basis of generators and motors.

## Making the needle

1. **Take a steel needle** (or a thin strip of spring steel — watch springs are ideal).
2. **Magnetize it**: stroke it repeatedly in one direction with a strong magnet
   (see [magnets](/dumb-humanity/tech/magnets)) — 50–100 strokes, always the same
   direction with the same pole.
3. **Test**: float it and check it aligns to north-south. If it sticks to the wrong
   pole of your magnet, flip it.

No magnet at hand? A steel needle hammered while aligned with the Earth's field can
pick up weak magnetization — less reliable, but workable.

## Two working designs

**Float compass (simplest):**
1. Push the needle through a small cork or wood chip.
2. Float it in a bowl of still water.
3. The needle turns to north-south. Mark the north end.

**Pivot compass (portable):**
1. Balance the needle on a sharp pivot (a pin in a brass cap) in a box with a marked
   dial.
2. Add a lubber line and a sight for taking bearings.

## Using and calibrating

- **Declination**: magnetic north is *not* true north. Find the local offset by
  sighting a known reference (the midday sun at noon points due south) and comparing —
  remember the correction, or mark it on the dial.
- Keep the compass away from iron objects and steel tools, which deflect it.
- Store it so the needle is not rubbing; a stuck needle reads wrong.

## Uses beyond direction

- **Setting out right angles** for buildings (90° bearings).
- **Mapping**: walking a bearing and pacing distances produces a surveyable map.
- **Telling the time** (sun + bearing) and rough latitude by the pole star.
