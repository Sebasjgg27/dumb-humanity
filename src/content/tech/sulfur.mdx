# Sulfur (Elemental)

Sulfur is the quiet lynchpin of the early chemical economy: **10% of every batch of
gunpowder**, the agent that vulcanizes rubber, and the raw material behind sulfuric
acid's cousins. It's cheap, ugly, and essential. Good news: the post-apocalyptic
world is rich in it.

## Why it matters on the ladder

- **Gunpowder** needs 10% sulfur as the ignition booster — no sulfur, no reliable
  powder (see [gunpowder](/dumb-humanity/tech/gunpowder)).
- **Vulcanization**: sulfur + heat + rubber → durable, weatherproof elastic. This is
  what made gaskets, hoses, and wheels possible.
- **Fungicides**: a lime-sulfur wash protects crops and treats skin parasites.
- It is a precursor of sulfate chemistry and useful for making matches.

## Sourcing it

**1. Native sulfur.** In volcanic regions, hot springs, and fumaroles you find bright
yellow crusts and crystals. Collect and scrape. Historically Sicily supplied the world
this way. If you are near a volcano or geothermal field, start here — it's nearly pure.

**2. From pyrite ("fool's gold").** Iron sulfide (FeS2) is common in many ores. The
retort method:

1. Crush pyrite into a sealed clay retort with a narrow outlet tube.
2. Heat strongly. Sulfur vapor comes off the outlet.
3. Condense it in a cold chamber — it deposits as "flowers of sulfur" (fine yellow
   powder) or, if melted and collected, as liquid sulfur.
4. The remaining iron in the retort is a usable iron-rich residue.

**3. From smelting byproducts.** When iron ores with sulfur are roasted or smelted,
sulfur dioxide gas (SO2) is driven off. You can bubble this into water to make sulfurous
acid, but for elemental sulfur the retort method is cleaner.

## Purifying it

Melt the sulfur gently (it melts at about **119 °C**, just below boiling water).
Molten sulfur is not miscible with water, so you can:

- Strain it through cloth to remove dirt.
- Pour off the clean molten layer; it doesn't wet surfaces, so it pools.

Do **not** overheat — liquid sulfur thickens and darkens if heated too fast.

## Handling and safety

- **Sulfur dioxide fumes are toxic.** Roasting pyrite and burning sulfur produce SO2 —
  work outdoors, upwind.
- Sulfur dust is **combustible**; keep it away from open flame and sparks.
- **Molten sulfur burns badly** and sticks to skin. Never reach into it.
- Store dry and sealed; damp sulfur still works but degrades.
- Do not confuse elemental sulfur with sulfides (e.g., pyrite) — you can't grind pyrite
  into gunpowder sulfur; it must be processed.
