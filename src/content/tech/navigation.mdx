# Navigation

Navigation is the art of answering two questions: **where am I** and **where am I
going**. With a compass and a clock you can answer both with real accuracy — and the
[clockwork](../clockwork/) guide is here precisely because the clock is
half of navigation.

## The two tools

- **Compass** (see [compass](../compass/)) — gives *direction*:
  a bearing to steer.
- **Clock** (see [clockwork](../clockwork/)) — gives *position along
  the line*: how far you've traveled and how the sky maps to your place on Earth.

## Latitude (how far north/south — easy)

The **pole star** sits almost exactly above the north pole of the sky. Its height above
the horizon, measured in degrees, equals your latitude. Without a sextant, a simple
**cross-staff** (a stick with a sliding crosspiece, sighted to the star) measures the
angle well enough to know your latitude within a degree or two.

- Measure the pole star with a cross-staff or improvised quadrant.
- The angle above the horizon = latitude (e.g., 40° at the 40th parallel).

## Longitude (how far east/west — hard)

Longitude is a time problem. The Earth turns 15° per hour, so:

- Compare **local noon** (when the sun is highest) with the time shown on your clock
  **set to a known reference** (e.g., your home port).
- Each hour of difference = 15° of longitude east or west.

This is exactly why an accurate, reliable clock (see
[clockwork](../clockwork/)) is a navigational instrument: without it,
longitude is a guess. This is the whole reason the "longitude prize" was the holy grail
of the 1700s.

## Dead reckoning (the working method)

When you can't see stars:

1. Hold a compass bearing.
2. Estimate speed and time elapsed (the clock again).
3. Plot the resulting course on a chart: *start + bearing + distance = position*.
4. Correct with any observed latitude you can grab.

This is how most of history navigated. It drifts, but it works.

## Making charts

- Record bearings, distances, and landmarks as you travel.
- Draw your own charts if none survive; note depths, hazards, and water sources.
- Log every voyage; a logbook is a shared memory that makes the next trip faster.

## Honest limitations

- Without a reliable clock, longitude is approximate — coastal sailing and latitudinal
  "running down" are safer than open-ocean crossing.
- Storms, currents, and drift defeat careless dead reckoning. Always overestimate
  distance to a coast.
- If you salvage a GPS: use it as a *check* on instruments, not a replacement — the
  battery dies and the satellites fail, but the compass and clock don't.

- [ ] Can you compute longitude from a clock and local noon?
